Connections

Connection 1 (Linear algebra: centroids and cosine similarity). SemanticIndex embeds its corpus, forms a recency-weighted centroid, and scores each new signal by cosine similarity to it (§36). Work in a toy 3-dimensional embedding space with orthonormal theme vectors a = (1, 0, 0), b = (0, 1, 0) and c = (0, 0, 1). (The real bge-small-en-v1.5 space has many more dimensions; the geometry is the same.) The corpus is: two commits on theme a, aged 0 and 7 days (14-day halflife); one active goal on theme b, aged 0 days (14-day halflife); one conversation turn on theme c, aged 3 days (3-day halflife). Use weight w = 2 to the power (-age / halflife). (a) Compute the centroid and the cosine of a, b, c with it. (b) Score the mixed signal s = (a + b) / sqrt(2). (c) Suppose a POSTMORTEM turn on theme a, aged 0 days, were included in the centroid instead of being excluded. Recompute the cosine for b. (d) What do (b) and (c) say about a single-centroid design?

Hint

Cosine similarity is scale-invariant, so you can work with the weighted sum and skip dividing by the total weight.

Worked solution

Weights. The commits get 1 and 2 to the power -0.5 = 0.7071. The goal gets 1. The conversation turn is exactly one halflife old, so it gets 0.5.

(a) The weighted sum is S = (1.7071, 1, 0.5). Dividing by the total weight 3.2071 gives the centroid (0.532, 0.312, 0.156), but cosine ignores that scale, so use S. Its norm is sqrt(2.914 + 1 + 0.25) = sqrt(4.164) = 2.041. Since a, b and c are unit vectors, each cosine is the matching component over the norm: cos(a) = 1.7071 / 2.041 = 0.837, cos(b) = 1 / 2.041 = 0.490, cos(c) = 0.5 / 2.041 = 0.245.

(b) The dot product of s with S is (1.7071 + 1) / sqrt(2) = 1.914, so cos(s) = 1.914 / 2.041 = 0.938. A signal halfway between two themes scores higher than a pure signal on the dominant theme (0.837). The centroid of a two-theme corpus lies between the themes, so it rewards "between" signals that may match no real piece of work.

(c) Adding a weight-1 vector on a gives S' = (2.7071, 1, 0.5), with norm sqrt(7.328 + 1 + 0.25) = 2.929. Now cos(b) = 1 / 2.929 = 0.341, down from 0.490, and cos(a) rises to 0.924. One failure record pulls the theme toward itself and pushes unrelated declared work down. That is the "failure gravity" §36 excludes postmortems to avoid.

(d) If the boost is taken as proportional to the non-negative cosine and capped at JARVIS_SEMANTIC_ALIGNMENT_BOOST_MAX = 1 (the exact mapping is an assumption here), signal s gets the largest boost and pure-b work gets about half of it. A single centroid averages several themes into one direction that may not exist in the corpus. Clustering fixes this: with one centroid per theme, a pure-b signal would score 1.0 against its own cluster. The paper later mentions a k-means cluster classifier with silhouette-based choice of K alongside the centroid (§92), which is the natural remedy. The cap of 1, below the hard goal boost of 2, is what keeps this geometric bias from starving declared goals.

Connection 2 (Control theory: exponential filters and feedback). SemanticIndex weights items by 2 to the power (-age / halflife), with a 14-day halflife for commits and goals and a 3-day halflife for conversation, over the last 30 commits (§36). (a) Convert each halflife to a time constant. (b) Treat the decay as a continuous low-pass filter over a steady stream of work. If the team switches theme abruptly, what share of total weight has the new theme after 3 days and after 14 days? (c) Now apply the 30-commit cap. Under a stated assumption of 10 commits per day, what share of weight does the new theme hold after 15 new commits? What about at 1 commit per day? (d) §36.1 describes a session focused on one bug making every later signal look like more of that bug. Identify the feedback loop, and what in §36 limits its gain. (The commit rates in (c) are illustrative assumptions, not figures from the paper.)

Hint

The time constant of 2 to the power (-t / h) is h / ln 2. For a constant input rate, the weight of everything newer than t days is a fraction 1 - 2 to the power (-t / h) of the total.

Worked solution

(a) tau = h / ln 2. For commits and goals, tau = 14 / 0.693 = 20.2 days. For conversation, tau = 3 / 0.693 = 4.3 days.

(b) Integrating a constant rate against the decay curve, the newest t days carry a share 1 - 2 to the power (-t / 14) of the weight. After 3 days that is 1 - 0.862 = 0.138. After 14 days it is exactly 0.5. Taken alone, the 14-day filter is sluggish: two weeks pass before a new theme outweighs the old one.

(c) At 10 commits per day, the 30-commit window spans only 3 days. After 15 new commits (1.5 days), the window holds 15 new and 15 old commits. The new commits have ages 0 to 1.5 days, with mean weight about 2 to the power (-0.75 / 14) = 0.963. The old ones have ages 1.5 to 3 days, with mean weight about 2 to the power (-2.25 / 14) = 0.895. The new share is 0.963 / (0.963 + 0.895) = 0.52. Here the count cap, not the halflife, sets the response time. At 1 commit per day, the window spans 30 days and the oldest commit weighs 2 to the power (-30 / 14) = 0.23, so the halflife dominates and the response is close to the slow filter in (b). Which mechanism sets the bandwidth depends on the commit rate.

(d) The loop is: the centroid boosts intake priority for signals near the current theme; boosted work produces commits on that theme; those commits enter the corpus and pull the centroid further toward the theme. That is positive feedback, which can lock a theme in. Three things in §36 limit its gain. The boost is clamped to at most 1, strictly below the hard goal boost of 2, so inferred themes cannot outrank declared goals. POSTMORTEM turns are excluded, so failures do not feed the loop. And the decay makes old reinforcement fade. The loop is bounded, not removed: within the cap it still favours the current theme.

Connection 3 (Probability and statistics: small samples and base rates). MemoryEngine raises a file's risk tier when its success rate is below 0.5 (§27). The paper does not say whether a minimum number of operations is required first. As stated assumptions for illustration: a healthy file succeeds independently with probability 0.8 per operation, a genuinely bad file with probability 0.3, and 10% of files that reach the check are genuinely bad. (a) For a healthy file, find the probability that its observed rate is below 0.5 after n = 1, 2, 3 and 10 operations. (b) At n = 3, what is the chance of flagging a bad file? (c) At n = 3, what fraction of flagged files are actually bad? (d) What does this imply for the threshold and for the 168-hour TTL?

Hint

"Below 0.5" is strict: with n = 2, one success out of two is exactly 0.5 and does not flag. Use the binomial distribution, then Bayes' rule for (c).

Worked solution

(a) With success probability 0.8: at n = 1 the file flags on a single failure, probability 0.2. At n = 2 it needs zero successes, 0.2 squared = 0.04. At n = 3 it needs at most one success: 0.2 cubed + 3 times 0.8 times 0.2 squared = 0.008 + 0.096 = 0.104. At n = 10 it needs at most four successes; summing the binomial terms gives 0.0000001 + 0.000004 + 0.000074 + 0.000786 + 0.005505, which is about 0.0064. The false-alarm rate does not fall smoothly with n (0.2, 0.04, 0.104), because the strict threshold interacts with small integer counts.

(b) With success probability 0.3 at n = 3: 0.7 cubed + 3 times 0.3 times 0.7 squared = 0.343 + 0.441 = 0.784.

(c) Bad and flagged: 0.1 times 0.784 = 0.0784. Healthy and flagged: 0.9 times 0.104 = 0.0936. So P(bad given flagged) = 0.0784 / (0.0784 + 0.0936) = 0.0784 / 0.172 = 0.46. Under these assumptions, more than half of the flags at n = 3 land on healthy files, because healthy files are so much more common.

(d) A raw rate on few operations is a noisy signal. A minimum sample size, or a confidence bound such as the lower end of an interval on the success rate, would cut false alarms sharply (at n = 10 the healthy-file rate falls to about 0.6%). The 168-hour TTL works against this: by expiring old records, it keeps n small for files that are touched rarely. The paper's design tolerates the noise because, as §28.2 says, a false positive costs only an extra review. The confidence cap of 0.6 on ProphecyEngine is the same honesty applied to the derived score (§28). Whether the real false-alarm rate is acceptable could be measured from file_reputations.json; the paper does not report it.
