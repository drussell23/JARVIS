Connections

Connection 1 (Physics of the hardware: VRAM arithmetic behind the lease). From the paper: the 30B needs about 21 GiB for inference and a GRPO step about 28 GiB (§94.5); the card is a 32 GB RTX 5090, which §94.6 also calls "a 32 GiB card"; the base has 30.5B parameters; Reactor's per-expert NF4 load lands it at 15.64 GiB resident (§93.11); stock 4-bit loading that left the experts in bf16 needed "about 54 GiB"; merging an adapter needs the bf16 base, 57 GiB, against a 47 GiB WSL guest (§94.6). Assume, from Qwen3-30B-A3B's published configuration and not from the paper: 48 layers, hidden size 2,048, expert intermediate size 768, three projections per expert (gate, up, down). Take the card as 32 GiB. (1) Show why serving and training cannot share the card, and how much is missing. (2) Check the 57 GiB merge figure and a pure 4-bit estimate against the 15.64 GiB measurement. (3) Count the expert parameters and use them to explain the "about 54 GiB". (4) What does the arithmetic say about the lease's design?

Hint

bf16 is 2 bytes per parameter and 4-bit is 0.5 bytes; 1 GiB is 230 bytes. One expert holds 3 × 2,048 × 768 weights, and there are 128 experts in each of 48 layers.

Worked solution

(1) 21 + 28 = 49 GiB against 32 GiB: short by 17 GiB, more than half a second card. Even with training alone resident, 32 − 28 = 4 GiB remains, about a fifth of what inference needs. No partial sharing works; one job must leave the card completely. Hence the lease: SERVING → DRAINING → RELEASED → RESTORING → SERVING (§94.5).

(2) bf16 base: 30.5 × 109 × 2 = 61.0 × 109 bytes = 56.8 GiB, which is the paper's 57 GiB. Merging cannot fit in a 47 GiB guest, which is why the converter writes an unmerged GGUF LoRA. Pure 4-bit: 30.5 × 109 × 0.5 = 15.25 × 109 bytes = 14.2 GiB. The measured 15.64 GiB is about 1.4 GiB higher. The paper does not break that down; plausible contributors are NF4's per-block scale constants and layers that are not quantized (embeddings, norms, routers). That is an inference, not a measurement.

(3) One expert: 3 × 2,048 × 768 = 4,718,592 weights. All experts: 48 × 128 × 4,718,592 = 28.99 × 109, about 95% of the 30.5B. In bf16 that is 28.99 × 109 × 2 = 57.98 × 109 bytes = 54.0 GiB. So "about 54 GiB" is almost exactly the expert weights left in bf16: stock loading quantized only the 5% that was not in experts. That is why "the 'bnb-NF4 30B' was never 4-bit", and why moe_quant.py refuses to load if any expert stays bf16.

(4) The margins are large and one-sided: a 17 GiB shortfall cannot be closed by tuning batch size or KV cache. The design therefore makes the swap safe rather than trying to avoid it. It drains in-flight work, proves release with nvidia-smi instead of trusting a terminate signal, and restores on expiry only through measured admission. And because 49 GiB can never be co-resident, the organism exiting with code 69 when the lane is lent is not caution but arithmetic.

Connection 2 (Combinatorics and linear algebra: adapting the experts). §94.6 says the default LoRA is rank 16 on the 192 attention projections (13.4M parameters, about 27 MB), and that adapting the experts "would mean 18,432 projections and about 830M parameters", opt-in. §94.5 says the base routes each token to 8 of 128 experts per layer. Use the same assumed configuration as Connection 1 (48 layers, hidden size 2,048, expert intermediate size 768, three projections per expert). (1) Derive 18,432 and the 830M figure, and the adapter's size at 2 bytes per parameter. (2) How many distinct expert subsets can a token be routed to in one layer, and what fraction of expert weights does a token touch? (3) Assuming routing spreads tokens evenly across experts (an assumption), what share of the training tokens reaches any one expert's adapter? (4) Relate this to §93.11's measured training signal and say what it implies for the default.

Hint

A rank-r LoRA on a dout × din matrix has r(din + dout) parameters. The number of ways to choose 8 of 128 is the binomial coefficient C(128, 8).

Worked solution

(1) Projections: 48 layers × 128 experts × 3 = 18,432. Each expert projection is 2,048 × 768 or 768 × 2,048, so r(din + dout) = 16 × 2,816 = 45,056 parameters. Total: 18,432 × 45,056 = 830.5 × 106, matching "about 830M". At 2 bytes: about 1.66 GB, against about 27 MB for the attention-only adapter, roughly 62 times larger.

(2) C(128, 8) = 1,429,702,652,400, about 1.4 × 1012 possible expert subsets per layer. A token touches 8/128 = 6.25% of each layer's experts. As a cross-check, with about 29.0B expert parameters (Connection 1), active weights per token are about 1.5B non-expert plus 29.0B × 0.0625 ≈ 1.8B expert, roughly 3.3B, consistent with the "A3B" in the model's name.

(3) Each token updates the adapters of its 8 chosen experts in each layer. Under even routing, any one expert sees 8/128 = 1/16 of the tokens. Every expert adapter therefore learns from about 6% of the data that each attention adapter sees, since every token passes through attention.

(4) §93.11 records a very thin signal from the first cycle: three contributing answers, gradient norm about 1e-4, one greedy flip. Splitting that across experts would give each expert adapter about a sixteenth of it, while multiplying the trainable parameters by 62. In linear-algebra terms, more directions to fit with less data per direction. That supports the default of attention only, and suggests expert adaptation is worth trying only after the corpus carries real contrast (diff-format rows, a held-out gain, §93.15). The size also matters operationally: a 27 MB adapter is cheap to publish, version and roll back in J-Prime's registry; a 1.66 GB one is not. The even-routing figure is an assumption; real routers are uneven, which would starve some experts further.

Connection 3 (Graph theory: who calls whom). Build two directed graphs from §94.7. Nodes: O+V (the organism), H (the Training Lifecycle Handoff), J (J-Prime), R (Reactor's :8090 receiver), T (Reactor's trainer and converter, run by H), and E (the experience-stream directory with its landing-label ledger). In the call graph, draw an edge X → Y when X makes a request to Y or starts Y as a process. In the data-flow graph, draw an edge when data produced by X is consumed by Y. (1) List both edge sets. (2) Is the call graph acyclic? Give a topological order and name its sinks. (3) Find the cycle in the data-flow graph and the edge in it that no component triggers on its own. (4) Use reachability in the call graph to predict the "when one of them is down" table.

Hint

E is a directory, not a service: nothing calls it, things write to and read from it. For (4), a failure of Y can stall X only if X has a call path to Y.

Worked solution

(1) Call graph: O+V → J (generation over /api/chat or /v1/chat/completions, the served-identity query, the boot-time lease check; bring-up also tries to start J); H → J (lease acquire, renew and release, adapter publish, evaluation scope); H → T (subprocess). Reactor makes no call to J, and J makes no call to anyone. Data-flow graph: O+V → E (recorder rows and landing labels); E → R (tailing); E → T (the GRPO reader); T → H (adapter file, exit code, JSON report); H → J (the published GGUF); J → O+V (generations from the served adapter).

(2) The call graph is acyclic. One topological order is H, T, O+V, J (R and E are isolated). J and T are sinks; nothing waits on O+V or H. Because no two services call each other, no cycle of waits between services can form: a distributed deadlock between components would need a cycle in this graph.

(3) The data-flow graph has the cycle O+V → E → T → H → J → O+V. That is Loop 2: the organism's own work becomes the model that does its next work. The edge into H is not triggered by any component: automatic triggering (JARVIS_GRPO_AUTOTRAIN_ENABLED) is off, so the operator starts each cycle. The receiver R is not on the cycle at all, because ingestion does not trigger training (§94.6). The learning cycle is closed only through a human-started edge, which is the doll's governance appearing in the graph.

(4) Nodes with a call path to J are O+V and H, so J down stalls generation and training: "nothing to generate with". R has no inbound call edges, so R down stalls nothing: "the organism still works; it just stops learning", and E buffers the data until R returns. H down mid-cycle leaves only J's lease state, which expires through measured admission. O+V down affects nothing in the call graph, matching "J-Prime keeps serving; Reactor idles". One rule is not visible in either graph: H and O+V must not run together. That is a mutual-exclusion constraint enforced through J's lease state (H refuses beside a live organism; O+V exits 69 when the lane is lent), which is why it needs a separate mechanism rather than an edge.
