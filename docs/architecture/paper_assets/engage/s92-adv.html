Advanced conceptual questions

A1. Property (a) of the bounded-RSI conjecture in §92.17 says the shell's deterministic entropy never decreases, because every accepted patch only adds to it. Read that claim against what §93.8 and §93.12 found five months later. Does the evidence support (a), contradict it, or show that the quantity is badly defined?

The evidence mostly shows that the quantity is badly defined. §92.17 counts the shell as the constraints that exist: rules, detectors, AST pins and tests. §93 shows that existing and acting are different things. §93.12 found 131 DARK features, with the flag on and the module on disk but nothing in production importing it. §93.8 found VALIDATE rungs that existed but did nothing: 524 byte-identical retry iterations re-validated a frozen pool, and 532 micro-fix runs made zero repairs. Self-critique was dying on 38 of 38 operations.

Measured as code on disk, the shell grew. Measured as constraints actually applied to candidates, it was much smaller than it looked, and parts of it could switch off without any code being removed. Something did shrink as code, too: the inline VALIDATE twin and its kill switch were deleted (§93.8), even though that probably strengthened the shell by leaving one owner.

§93.3 adds a second problem. Property (b) is stated only "in the dimensions Sn now bounds". The at-scale escape rate of about 8.8% comes from runtime-built strings passed to eval, exec, subprocess and pickle, a dimension a static cage cannot bound by construction. So (b) is not contradicted, but it says nothing about the dimension that matters most.

My position is that (a) is only testable if deterministic entropy is defined over effective constraints. The reachability ledger's registered → invoked → effective states (§93.12) are the instrument for that. The evidence that would settle it is a time series of effective-constraint counts across soaks. Any drop, for example a capability going DARK, falsifies (a) as a claim about the running system, even if it holds for the source tree.

A2. §92.16 says the substrate is "positioned to close the SICA-empirical gap on a multi-month timeline", conditional on Phase 9 flag graduation. Use §93 to judge that forecast. What came true, what did not, and what does the difference say about the gap between "structurally ready" and "capable"?

Part of it came true. By 2026-10-10 there were 91 autonomous landings on main, five of them production code, and three of those modified files inside the governance package under signed goals (§93.10). That is well past the 23 ledger-write commits that were the whole empirical record in §92.16.

The rest did not happen. §93.10 says the system is still at Mechanic. No landing touched a safety gate, and none was self-initiated governance change, which is the SICA-style regime §92.16 had in view.

The bottleneck was also not the one the forecast named. §92.16 put the remaining gap on Phase 9 graduation, "not on novel research". In practice the blockers were: the A1 wall, where a signed goal never dispatched even with the system funded and quiet (§93.4); the paid credit running out (§93.5); a small local model that needed tool masking, a target symbol and a diff schema before it could act (§93.6, §93.8); and, once it could land, the supply wall and diff fidelity (§93.10, §93.14). None of those is a flag waiting to graduate.

The lesson is that "structurally ready" counts the mechanisms present. "Capable" depends on whether a real goal can travel the whole chain on the model you can actually afford. §93.12's 131 DARK features show how far apart the two can be. A forecast that would have survived needed a demonstrated end-to-end run as its base, not a list of present mechanisms.

A3. The SWE-Bench-Pro graduation floor in §92.14 requires at least one RESOLVED known-good instance and at least one UNRESOLVED known-hard instance. Why both ends? Then apply the same reasoning to the EVAL-2 sweep in §93.3, which resolved 1/6 with three instances inconclusive.

A scorer can be broken in two opposite ways, and each end of the floor catches one. A scorer that marks everything resolved would pass a known-good instance, but the known-hard instance exposes it. A scorer that marks everything unresolved, for example because tests never run in the container, would pass the known-hard instance, but the known-good one exposes it. Only the pair shows that the scorer can tell the two apart. §92.14 says this is "empirical proof that the scoring layer behaves correctly on both ends of the distribution before any aggregate-resolve-rate claim is published".

The same logic applies to inconclusive outcomes. Phase E turns contract violations into synthetic records such as INGEST_FAILED and SCORING_ERROR instead of aborting the sweep (§92.14). Those records are neither resolved nor unresolved. In EVAL-2, three of six instances were inconclusive because of a clone race and a provider outage (§93.3).

There are two readings. The reported 1/6 (16.7%) counts inconclusive instances as failures, which is conservative. Counting only conclusive instances gives 1/3. Neither can be compared with the 43.6% frontier figure, because the sample is tiny and half of it measured the infrastructure, not the agent. §93.3 itself calls the first resolved instance N=1. The honest report gives the three-way split (resolved, unresolved, inconclusive) and does not blend it into one rate.

Challenge problems

Challenge 1 (quantitative). §92.9 gives the Phase 9 calendar as 24 flags × 3 clean soaks ÷ 2 fires per day = about 36 days minimum, and "realistic 6–9 weeks with noise". Model the noise. Assume flags graduate one at a time, each soak is clean independently with probability p, and a flag needs 3 consecutive clean soaks, so a dirty soak resets its count. Compute the expected calendar for p = 0.9, 0.8 and 0.75. Find which values of p the paper's 6–9 week estimate implies, and give the cost ceiling at the stated rate.

Hint

The expected number of independent trials needed to get k consecutive successes, each with probability p, is 1/p + 1/p² + … + 1/pk.

Worked solution

Assumptions, which are mine and not the paper's: soaks are independent, flags are processed serially, and "3 clean soaks" means three in a row. §92.13 uses "consecutive" for Treefinement, so the stricter reading is reasonable. Every soak, clean or dirty, uses one of the two daily fires.

With k = 3, expected soaks per flag E(p) = 1/p + 1/p² + 1/p³.

  • p = 0.9: 1.111 + 1.235 + 1.372 = 3.72 soaks per flag. 24 × 3.72 = 89.2 soaks ÷ 2 per day = 44.6 days, about 6.4 weeks.
  • p = 0.8: 1.250 + 1.563 + 1.953 = 4.77. 24 × 4.77 = 114.4 soaks = 57.2 days, about 8.2 weeks.
  • p = 0.75: 1.333 + 1.778 + 2.370 = 5.48. 24 × 5.48 = 131.6 soaks = 65.8 days, about 9.4 weeks.

Check: at p = 1, E = 3, which gives 72 soaks and 36 days, the paper's minimum. So "6–9 weeks" corresponds to each soak being clean roughly 78–90% of the time. Below about 75% the calendar runs past nine weeks, and it grows quickly, because the 1/p³ term dominates.

Cost: §92.9 caps the cadence at about 1 US dollar per day. 36 days is at most about 36 US dollars, and 66 days about 66 US dollars. The cost is trivial. The binding resource is calendar time.

Conclusion: the paper's estimate is consistent with a fairly reliable soak, and the model shows where the risk is. Lowering the dirty-soak rate (the §92.10 debug arc is that kind of work) shortens the calendar more than anything else. Two fires per day is a hard ceiling. If a reset only cost one soak instead of the whole count, E would fall to 3/p, about 3.75 at p = 0.8.

Challenge 2 (adversarial). Treefinement graduates when a soak ladder shows "≥10% L2 success-rate lift OR ≥20% wall-clock reduction at parity success rate over ≥2 consecutive soaks" (§92.13). Without any real improvement from tree search, construct two event sequences that would meet this criterion. Use a typical soak of 20 L2 invocations per arm at a true success rate of 25%. For each, say whether anything in §92 would stop it, and propose a criterion that would.

Hint

Ask three things the criterion does not specify: relative or absolute lift, what the baseline arm is, and whether both arms see the same work under the same infrastructure. §93.10's slot A/B is a real example of one of these failing.

Worked solution

Sequence 1: chance plus an ambiguous threshold. With 20 invocations per arm at p = 0.25, the standard error of a difference in success rates is sqrt(2 × 0.25 × 0.75 / 20) ≈ 0.137, or about 14 points. Using a normal approximation (mine), a 10-point absolute lift appears by chance with probability P(Z ≥ 0.73) ≈ 0.23 per soak, or about 0.054 for two soaks in a row. Read as a 10% relative lift, the bar is only 2.5 points: P(Z ≥ 0.18) ≈ 0.43 per soak and about 0.18 for two. So a criterion with no stated sample size or definition can graduate a no-op roughly one time in five.

Sequence 2: a confounded comparison. The tree arm runs after an unrelated infrastructure fix, or on an easier goal mix. §93.2's Quiescence Protocol cut first-token latency from 94,638 ms to 722 ms without any change to repair strategy. A change like that, landing between a baseline soak and a tree soak, would deliver the "≥20% wall-clock reduction at parity" on its own. §93.10 shows the goal-mix confound happening for real: the slot A/B arms had different goal mixes and the result was inconclusive.

What stops it. Nothing in §92 does. The flag is default-FALSE and rollback is byte-identical, so a false graduation is reversible, but the ladder itself would pass. That is an honest gap.

Proposed criterion (my proposal). (1) Paired arms: replay the same failing operations under JARVIS_L2_BRANCHING_STRATEGY=linear and under tree mode on the same commit of the infrastructure, so goal mix and latency are identical. (2) An absolute threshold and a minimum count of L2 invocations, fixed before the soak. (3) A paired test on per-operation outcomes, where only operations that one strategy repairs and the other does not count as evidence. This is the same discipline §93.11 later applied to adapters, where a 4/6-versus-3/6 gate result was explicitly rejected as evidence because it rested on one flip.

Check: under the proposal, Sequence 2 is impossible by construction, and Sequence 1 needs several discordant operations in the same direction, not a few points of noise.
