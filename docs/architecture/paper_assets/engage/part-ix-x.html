Connections

Connection 1 (Queueing theory). The router holds at most 16 envelopes and evicts the lowest-priority one when full (§56.1). Model the intake queue as a single server that runs one operation at a time. These are assumptions, not facts from the paper: envelopes that survive storm protection arrive as a Poisson stream, and service times are exponential with a mean of 10 minutes. The 10 minutes is rounded up from Session W, whose op ran at least 524s from VALIDATE to its last DECISION (§65.4). (a) With 4 envelopes per hour, find the utilisation, the mean wait before dispatch, and how often 16 or more envelopes are waiting. (b) With 12 envelopes per hour, what happens to the queue? Use Little's law to find the mean time an envelope spends in the full queue. (c) What would doubling the capacity to 32 slots buy in case (b)?

Hint

For an M/M/1 queue with utilisation rho, the chance of n or more envelopes in the system is rho to the power n. Little's law, L = lambda x W, holds for any stable system, whatever the distributions, if lambda is the rate at which items leave.

Worked solution

(a) The service rate is mu = 6 per hour. The utilisation is rho = 4/6 = 2/3. The mean wait before dispatch is Wq = rho / (mu - lambda) = (2/3) / 2 = 1/3 hour, or 20 minutes. Sixteen or more waiting means 17 or more in the system, one being served: (2/3)^17 is about 0.001. The cap binds about 0.1% of the time, so below saturation the 16-slot limit almost never evicts anything.

(b) Now rho = 12/6 = 2. The server cannot keep up, so the queue fills to 16 and stays full. Dispatches are capped at mu = 6 per hour, so in steady state about 12 - 6 = 6 envelopes per hour are evicted, half of all arrivals. Little's law on the queue: L = 16, and envelopes leave at 12 per hour (6 dispatched, 6 evicted). The mean time in queue is 16 / 12 hours, about 80 minutes. That is an average over all envelopes. Under priority eviction, high-priority envelopes go out quickly and low-priority ones are the ones evicted.

(c) Throughput stays at mu = 6 per hour, because the server is the bottleneck. With 32 slots, L = 32 and the mean time in queue becomes 32 / 12 hours, about 160 minutes. Evictions still run at about 6 per hour once the larger queue fills. Doubling the capacity doubles the delay and buys no extra work done.

What it says about the architecture. The bounded queue is a storm defence, not a throughput device. Below saturation it is invisible. Above saturation it turns excess load into evictions of the least important work, and it puts a ceiling on waiting time of capacity divided by throughput. The only lever that raises the number of operations completed is the service rate: shorter ops or more concurrent workers. This is also why the paper treats eviction as recoverable: an evicted envelope may be re-ingested if its sensor fires again (§56.1).

Connection 2 (Combinatorics: the birthday bound). Storm-protection layer 2 drops an event if its SHA-256 fingerprint matches one seen in the last 60 seconds (§55.2). Dedup by fingerprint can go wrong in one direction: two different events with the same fingerprint, where the second is silently dropped. Take as given the published specification that SHA-256 outputs 256 bits. (a) Suppose an extreme storm puts one million distinct events into a single 60-second window. Estimate the chance that any two share a fingerprint. (b) Repeat with a hypothetical 32-bit fingerprint, for 1,000 and then 100,000 events in the window. (c) What does the result say about the errors this layer can and cannot make?

Hint

With n items hashed uniformly into N values, there are about n^2/2 pairs. The chance of any collision is about 1 - e^(-n^2 / 2N), which is close to n^2 / 2N when that number is small.

Worked solution

(a) N = 2^256, about 1.16e77. With n = 1e6 there are about 5e11 pairs. The chance is about 5e11 / 1.16e77 = 4.3e-66. That is zero for any practical purpose, even at a storm rate no repository produces.

(b) With a 32-bit fingerprint, N = 2^32, about 4.29e9. At n = 1,000 there are 499,500 pairs, and the chance is about 499,500 / 4.29e9 = 1.2e-4, already noticeable over many windows. At n = 100,000, n^2 / 2N = 5e9 / 4.29e9 = 1.16, so the chance is 1 - e^(-1.16), about 0.69. A short fingerprint would silently drop real events during exactly the storms the layer exists for.

(c) With 256 bits, the layer effectively never merges two different events. Its only practical error runs the other way. Two events that mean the same thing, but differ in even one byte of whatever is fingerprinted, hash to unrelated values and both pass. Layer 2 therefore catches only exact repeats inside its 60s window. That is why the other three layers judge sameness differently: FileWatchGuard debounces by time and dedups by checksum, TestFailureSensor debounces by time, and the router dedups on a dedup_key chosen at the envelope level (§55.2). The paper calls the layers orthogonal, and the birthday bound shows why that is necessary: a cryptographic fingerprint is a near-perfect test of identity and no test of meaning.
