Connections

Connection 1 (Physics of the hardware: the memory-bandwidth roofline). When decoding a single stream, each new token requires reading the model's active weights from GPU memory once, so decode speed is bounded by memory bandwidth. Assume, as givens not stated in the paper: the RTX 5090's published memory bandwidth of about 1.79 TB/s, and a served 4-bit k-quant (Q4_K_M class) of about 4.5 to 5 bits per weight. From the paper, take Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B's 3B active parameters, the measured decode p50 of 172 tok/s at one slot, and 18.9 GiB resident after cold load (§93.6). (1) Compute the bandwidth ceiling on single-stream decode. (2) Express the measured p50 as a fraction of that ceiling and as per-token time. (3) Compare with a dense model that reads all ~30B parameters per token, and with reading the entire 18.9 GiB per token. (4) Explain the gap.

Hint

Bytes per token = active parameters × bits per weight ÷ 8. The ceiling is bandwidth ÷ bytes per token. For the gap, compare 1/172 s with the time the weight reads alone would take.

Worked solution

(1) Bytes per token = 3 × 109 × 4.5/8 ≈ 1.69 GB, up to 3 × 109 × 5/8 ≈ 1.88 GB. The ceiling is 1.79 × 1012 ÷ 1.69 × 109 ≈ 1,060 tok/s, down to 1.79 × 1012 ÷ 1.88 × 109 ≈ 955 tok/s. So the ceiling is roughly 950 to 1,060 tok/s. This is a derived upper bound, not a measurement.

(2) 172 ÷ 1,060 ≈ 16%, and 172 ÷ 955 ≈ 18%. Per token, the measured time is 1/172 ≈ 5.8 ms, while the weight reads alone need about 0.94 to 1.05 ms. Roughly 4.8 ms of each token goes to something other than streaming active weights.

(3) A dense model of the same size at about 4.75 bits per weight reads 30 × 109 × 4.75/8 ≈ 17.8 GB per token, which caps it at about 100 tok/s. Reading the whole resident footprint (18.9 GiB ≈ 20.3 GB) caps at about 88 tok/s. The measured 172 tok/s is above both bounds. The measurement therefore shows that decode really touches only a sparse subset of the weights: the mixture-of-experts design is what makes 172 tok/s physically possible on this card. (The footprint is also consistent with the assumed quantization: 30 × 109 × 4.75/8 ≈ 17.8 GB of weights, with the remainder plausibly KV cache and buffers.)

(4) The paper does not break down the 5.8 ms, so these are candidate causes, not measured ones. First, KV-cache reads: attention reads cached keys and values for the whole context every token, which grows with context up to the 32,768 window. Second, sparse expert reads: routed reads hit scattered, smaller matrices, which use bandwidth less efficiently than one large contiguous read. Third, fixed per-token costs: many small kernel launches per layer, routing, sampling and host synchronisation, which matter more as the bytes per token shrink.

The slot sweep (Connection 2) is consistent with this. If decode were purely limited by weight bandwidth, a second slot would share the same weight reads and nearly double aggregate throughput. It added only 33%.

For the architecture: the 5090 is far from its bandwidth roofline for one stream. Gains are more likely to come from batching and per-token overhead than from a faster memory system. And the choice of a 3B-active model over a dense 32B is what moved the ceiling from about 100 tok/s to about 1,000.

Connection 2 (Queueing theory). §93.6 reports a slot sweep with aggregate throughput of 124, 165 and 177 tok/s at 1, 2 and 3 slots, with "the knee at 2". Assume, as a round figure not taken from the paper, that each generation call emits 2,000 tokens and that every slot is kept busy. (1) For each slot count, compute per-stream speed, time per call, and calls completed per hour, and check the 3-slot case with Little's law. (2) Compute the marginal gain of each added slot and the latency cost per call. (3) Compare decode capacity with the cadence table in §93.10 and the supply wall. What can and cannot be concluded about whether slots are the bottleneck?

Hint

Per-stream speed = aggregate ÷ slots. Little's law: L = λW, where L is calls in flight, λ is completions per second and W is time per call. For (3), ask what happens to throughput when work arrives more slowly than the servers can handle it.

Worked solution

(1) One slot: 124 tok/s per stream. A call takes 2,000/124 ≈ 16.1 s, so about 223 calls per hour. Two slots: 82.5 tok/s per stream, 24.2 s per call, and 165 × 3,600/2,000 ≈ 297 calls per hour. Three slots: 59 tok/s per stream, 33.9 s per call, and about 319 calls per hour. Little's law check at 3 slots: λ = 177/2,000 ≈ 0.0885 calls/s, and λW = 0.0885 × 33.9 ≈ 3.0 = L.

(2) The second slot adds 41 tok/s (+33%). The third adds 12 tok/s (+7%). Scaling efficiency is 1.33/2 ≈ 67% at two slots and 1.43/3 ≈ 48% at three. Each call also gets slower: 24.2/16.1 ≈ 1.5 times at two slots, and 33.9/24.2 ≈ 1.4 times again at three. The third slot buys 7% more output for 40% more latency per call, against pipeline deadlines derived from one wall-clock budget (§93.12). That is what "knee at 2" means.

Note that the 1-slot sweep figure (124) is below the 172 tok/s decode p50. The paper does not reconcile the two. A plausible reason is that one is aggregate output over the sweep, including prefill and gaps, and the other is per-request decode speed. That is an inference, not a reported fact.

(3) In queueing terms, if landable work arrives at rate λa below the service capacity μ, then throughput equals λa. Extra servers only shorten waits. The slot count can change decode capacity by at most 43% (124 to 177). Yet within the single 10-05 soak, at a constant slot count, yield fell from about 1.34 substantive landings per hour to about 0.66, and nothing landed from 22:24 to 00:29 (§93.10). Capacity did not change, so that drop cannot come from decode. It matches the supply wall: on uncovered modules, after about four hours, the queue of landable work runs dry.

What cannot be concluded: the paper does not give generation calls per landing (exploration rounds, sibling candidates, retries). So it is not known whether decode binds early in a soak, before supply runs out. That is exactly why §93.14 calls slot scaling unproven and §93.15 asks for a complete three-arm A/B on the same goal mix. For the architecture, raising supply (step 3 of §93.15) matters more than adding a third slot.

Connection 3 (Linear algebra: LoRA as a low-rank update). LoRA leaves a weight matrix W (dout × din) frozen and trains an update ΔW = BA, where B is dout × r and A is r × din. §93.11 says Reactor's LoRA covers attention only, with 13.4M trainable parameters and an adapter of about 27 MB. The paper does not state the rank or the model's dimensions. Assume the published configuration of Qwen3-30B-A3B: 48 layers, hidden size 2,048, 32 query heads and 4 key-value heads, each of head dimension 128. Adapt the four attention projections q, k, v and o. (1) Write the trainable-parameter count as a function of r and solve for r. (2) Check the 27 MB figure. (3) Compare with fully fine-tuning the same matrices. (4) Explain why low rank keeps the adapter small, and what it gives up.

Hint

A rank-r adapter on a dout × din matrix has r(din + dout) parameters. Work out the q, k, v and o shapes from the head counts: q is 2,048 → 32 × 128, and k and v are 2,048 → 4 × 128.

Worked solution

(1) The shapes are: q maps 2,048 → 4,096; k maps 2,048 → 512; v maps 2,048 → 512; o maps 4,096 → 2,048. The values of din + dout are 6,144, 2,560, 2,560 and 6,144, which sum to 17,408 per layer per unit of rank. Across 48 layers that is 835,584r. Setting 835,584r = 13.4 × 106 gives r ≈ 16.0. At r = 16 the count is 13,369,344, which matches the paper's 13.4M. The rank is derived here under the assumed dimensions. The paper does not report it.

(2) 13.37 × 106 parameters × 2 bytes (16-bit storage) ≈ 26.7 MB, which matches "about a 27 MB adapter". So the adapter is stored at 16 bits per parameter.

(3) The full matrices hold 2,048 × 4,096 ≈ 8.39M parameters for q, 1.05M each for k and v, and 8.39M for o. That is about 18.9M per layer, or about 906M across 48 layers. The adapter is 13.4/906 ≈ 1.5% of the attention weights, and about 0.045% of the 30B model. The mixture-of-experts feed-forward weights, the bulk of the model, are not adapted at all.

(4) A full update to a d × d matrix needs d2 numbers. A rank-r update needs 2rd, which is linear in d, not quadratic. For q, the ratio is 16 × 6,144 / (2,048 × 4,096) ≈ 1.2%. That is why the adapter is small enough to convert to GGUF and to publish, activate or roll back in J-Prime's registry as a separate artifact while the base weights stay fixed (§93.6, §93.11).

The cost is expressiveness. ΔW has rank at most 16, so each adapted matrix can change its output in at most 16 directions of its input space. The current results do not show that this limit is what binds. The paper's own diagnosis points elsewhere: three contributing answers, a gradient norm of about 1e-4, and 86% of prompts unable to fit their whole-file answer (§93.11). With that little signal, a higher rank would mostly add parameters with nothing to learn from. The architecture's next lever is the data and the diff contract, not the adapter's rank.
